Practice from a jazz book you already own

What you already know gets played the most. The rest waits. Type each line you are working on, one example per line. Play keeps time. Next brings the next one. Each example comes up once, then the order changes.

The books below stay the books. Buy or borrow the legal edition. These pages name them so you can practice a line you already have. They do not reprint the exercises.

  • Scales and pitch sets

Melodic lines

One cell, pattern, or short line per example.

How to practice melodic lines

  • Jerry Bergonzi, Inside Improvisation Vol. 1: Melodic Structures

    One short melodic cell per line, in one key.

  • Jerry Bergonzi, Inside Improvisation Vol. 3: Jazz Line

    One phrase shape per line.

  • Jerry Bergonzi, Inside Improvisation Vol. 5: Thesaurus of Intervallic Melodies

    One interval melody per line.

  • Jerry Bergonzi, Inside Improvisation Vol. 6: Developing a Jazz Language

    One sentence you can sing per line.

  • Jamey Aebersold, Vol. 1: How to Play Jazz and Improvise

    One scale, chord, or short pattern from the chapter you are on.

  • Dan Haerle, The Jazz Language

    One phrase shape per line, in one key.

  • Jerry Coker and others, Patterns for Jazz

    One pattern you are memorizing per line.

  • Bert Ligon, Comprehensive Technique for Jazz Musicians

    One technical pattern per line.

  • Scott Reeves, Creative Jazz Improvisation

    One motive or short line from the chapter you are on.

  • Jerry Coker, Elements of the Jazz Language for the Developing Improvisor

    One language element you are learning to hear, per line.

  • David Baker, How to Play Bebop, volumes 1–3

    One bebop device per line, in one key.

  • David Baker, Jazz Improvisation: A Comprehensive Method

    One pattern, chord-tone path, or short line from the chapter you are in.

  • Bert Ligon, Connecting Chords with Linear Harmony

    One line that joins two chords.

  • Shelton Berg, Jazz Improvisation: The Goal-Note Method

    One path into a goal note per line.

  • Joe Riposo, Target and Approach Tones: Shaping Bebop Lines

    One approach into a target tone per line.

  • Joe Riposo, Developing a Jazz Vocabulary

    One vocabulary phrase per line.

  • Ed Byrne, Linear Jazz Improvisation

    One linear idea per line.

  • John McNeil, The Art of Jazz Trumpet

    One line shape per line, on any instrument.

  • Oliver Nelson, Patterns for Improvisation

    One pattern you are learning per line, in one key.

  • Nicolas Slonimsky, Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns

    One melodic pattern per line. Leave the rest of the book closed.

  • Hal Galper, Forward Motion

    One phrase that aims at the next chord.

  • Gary Campbell, Expansions: A Method for Developing New Material

    One developed motive per line.

  • Yusef Lateef, Repository of Scales and Melodic Patterns

    One scale or one melodic pattern per line.

  • John O’Gallagher, Twelve-Tone Improvisation

    One row shape you are practicing per line.

  • Wayne Krantz, An Improviser’s OS

    One rule you keep for those bars, per line.

  • Mick Goodrick, The Advancing Guitarist

    One voicing or one moving voice per line.

  • Miles Okazaki, Fundamentals of Guitar

    One path of pitches or one rhythmic grid per line.

Scales and pitch sets

One scale, pentatonic, hexatonic, triad pair, or interval set per example.

How to practice scales and pitch sets

  • Jerry Bergonzi, Inside Improvisation Vol. 2: Pentatonics

    One pentatonic color per line.

  • Jerry Bergonzi, Inside Improvisation Vol. 7: Hexatonics

    One six-note set per line.

  • David Liebman, A Chromatic Approach to Jazz Harmony and Melody

    One chromatic path into a chord tone per line.

  • David Liebman and Matt Vashlishan, How to Approach Standards Chromatically

    One section of the tune per line.

  • Mark Levine, The Jazz Theory Book

    One scale or mode from the chapter you are in. Progressions from this book are under chord changes.

  • Ramon Ricker, Pentatonic Scales for Jazz Improvisation

    One pentatonic pattern per line, in one key.

  • Gary Campbell, Triad Pairs for Jazz

    One triad pair per line.

  • Walt Weiskopf, Around the Horn

    One pattern per line in the key you are on.

  • Walt Weiskopf, Intervallic Improvisation

    One interval pattern per line.

  • Walt Weiskopf and Ed Rosenman, Beyond the Horn

    One pattern per line, then the next key as its own exercise.

  • Tim Murphy, Triad Pairs: The First Step

    One pair per line, the shapes you are learning first.

  • Ramon Ricker, Technique Development in Fourths for Jazz Improvisation

    One fourths pattern per line.

  • George Russell, The Lydian Chromatic Concept of Tonal Organization

    One scale color you are learning to hear, per line.

    One soloing target per line.

  • John A. Elliott, Insights in Jazz

    One section of a tune per line.

  • Conrad Cork, Harmony with LEGO Bricks

    One short progression you can move through the keys.

  • Mark Levine, The Jazz Theory Book

    One progression from the chapter you are in.

  • Alan Kingstone, The Barry Harris Harmonic Method

    One harmonic move per line, on one degree.

Time

One rhythm, polyrhythm, or metric idea per example.

How to practice time

  • Jerry Bergonzi, Inside Improvisation Vol. 4: Melodic Rhythms

    One rhythmic placement of a melody per line.

  • Hal Crook, Beyond Time and Changes

    One metric idea or one free-time cue per line. Picture cues from this book are under free playing.

  • Peter Erskine, Time Awareness for All Musicians

    One time grid you can tap, per line.

  • Ari Hoenig and Johannes Weidenmueller, Intro to Polyrhythms

    One polyrhythm per line, for a full cycle of the bars.

  • Ed Saindon, Exploration in Rhythm

    One rhythm you are exploring per line.

Sound and attention

One sound goal or one attention cue per example.

Open Sonic Nuances

  • David Liebman, Developing a Personal Saxophone Sound

    One sound goal per line: air, soft, loud, or a color you can hear.

  • Kenny Werner, Effortless Mastery

    One attention cue per line, such as sing it first, or play it soft. Picture cues from this book are under free playing.

Free playing

One picture per example: weather, a feeling, or a short story. Any symbol you can type, including emoji.

How to practice free playing

  • Kenny Werner, Effortless Mastery

    One picture of attention per line: space, softness, or a single note.

  • Hal Crook, Beyond Time and Changes

    One free cue per line: weather, a burst, a long tone, or silence.

  • Stephen Nachmanovitch, Free Play: Improvisation in Life and Art

    One picture of play per line: a joke, a goodbye, a story, or an accident you keep.
