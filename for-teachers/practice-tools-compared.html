Metronome App vs Flashcards vs Backing Track vs eization

What each tool is actually for. Categories, not dunk contests. eization does not win every job.

By Błażej Drobniuch · 28 Sep 2026 · 8 min read

Teachers collect tools the way guitarists collect cables: hopefully useful, often tangled. This page compares four categories you already use or recommend—plain metronome apps, flashcard-style recall tools, backing tracks (including play-along apps), and eization—by job, not by brand loyalty.

Buying another metronome will not make Tuesday’s lesson go better. Matching the tool to the job might.

At a glance

Jobs, not winners. Use more than one tool in a week.
Tool Strong at Weak at
Metronome apps Pure timing, subdivision, session habits Deciding what to play next; coverage of a list
Flashcards / spaced recall Names, spellings, theory facts, vocabulary Playing in tempo; musical feel under a click
Backing tracks Musical context, motivation, form Honest exposure of weak spots; easy to hide in the changes
eization One example at a time + metronome; full-set coverage Band simulation; notation; full curriculum delivery

Metronome apps

Pros: Best pure timing tool. Subdivisions, accents, and “don’t rush the and of two” remain eternal. Low ceremony. Works for any instrument.

Cons: The click does not care whether you looped the same three bars for twenty minutes. Weak at “what to play next.” Skins and features multiply; the practice problem often does not.

Use when: tempo is the lesson. Technique under a steady grid. Ensemble prep where everyone must share one pulse.

Flashcards and spaced recall

Pros: Excellent for recall—chord spellings, fingerings as facts, term definitions, language syllables. Spaced repetition is a real study method for memory.

Cons: Awkward for playing in tempo. A correct tap on “Dorian” is not the same as eight clean bars in time. Easy to confuse knowing about music with playing music.

Use when: the bottleneck is naming or remembering, not time feel. Then move the same material into an exercise under a click.

Backing tracks and play-alongs

Pros: Musical and motivating. Form, groove, and “I sound like I’m in a band” keep students in the chair. Great for applying vocabulary after it is solid.

Cons: Students can hide in the changes. Weak spots get papered over by a good drummer in their headphones. Setup and key/tempo hunting can eat the warm-up. Not every track is honest about time.

Use when: the example already works alone with a metronome, and you want context. Do not use a backing track as the first place a new line meets the clock.

eization

Pros: Built for one example on screen with the metronome. Each example in the exercise comes up once; then the order changes. Custom lists, share links, stock exercises, device storage without an account. Free to use in the browser. Screen tries to stay awake while the practice page is in front.

Cons: Not a band-in-a-box. Not notation software. Not a graded curriculum or a listening AI that judges the take. Will not replace your ear. If the student needs harmonic wallpaper more than coverage, pick a backing track.

Use when: the lesson goal is coverage under time—scales across keys, a finite set of lines, rhythm cells, tone prompts—without the student vetoing the awkward example. See how to practice and lesson plans.

A simple studio rule

  • Learn the fact → flashcards if needed.
  • Play the example in time alone → metronome or eization.
  • Cover the whole set without favorites eating the hour → eization.
  • Apply in musical context → backing track.

Those are complementary jobs. Treating them as rivals is how you end up with five apps and the same Measure 3 problem you had last year.
