The Student Who Only Ever Practiced Measure 3

A short story about the bar that feels good, the ones that do not, and the week the order stopped being a negotiation.

By Błażej Drobniuch · 14 Jul 2026 · 7 min read

Every studio has a Measure 3.

Sometimes it is literally bar three of an etude. Sometimes it is the A section of Autumn Leaves before the bridge shows up and the student’s face rearranges itself into polite panic. Sometimes it is C major on the saxophone, or open strings on the guitar, or the one scale finger pattern that somehow became the national anthem of Tuesday’s warm-up.

Measure 3 is the place practice goes when nobody is watching the map.

The week the assignment was clear

I once taught a student—call him M.—who could play the first phrase of a bebop head as if it owed him money. Clean time. Nice sound. He would start the metronome, play that phrase, smile the small smile of someone who has paid rent to the music, and then… restart.

I had written the homework in the notebook like a grown-up: all six phrases, slow, with the click. He nodded. He meant it. He practiced for forty minutes. On Thursday he could still only play phrase one.

“I did practice,” he said, and I believed him. He had practiced the feeling of being good at music. Phrase one is very good at that.

“I practiced for an hour” often means “I visited the same comfortable bar forty times.”

We tried stickers. We tried a different book. We tried me standing there like a traffic cone while he played. We tried the motivational speech that starts with “in the real world the bridge still comes.” The bridge remained undefeated.

What the metronome will not do for you

Teachers love metronomes, and for good reason. Time does not negotiate. But a metronome will happily keep perfect eighth notes while a student loops the same three bars of glory. The click is honest about tempo. It is silent about coverage.

Flashcards help memory. Backing tracks make the room feel like a band. None of those tools force the awkward example to the front of the line. Students still choose the order. Students choose Measure 3.

If you have ever watched someone “run scales” by starting every day on concert C and somehow never arriving at F♯, you already know the plot.

The Tuesday we changed the queue

I put each phrase on its own line. One example per line. Six lines. We set the metronome. One example on the screen. Play kept time. When the bars filled, or when he hit Next, the next example came up—and it was not always the one he would have voted for.

He hated it for about twelve minutes. That is normal. Measure 3 had been the mayor of his practice town, and we had just held an election.

By the end of the lesson he could play phrase four at a tempo that would not impress anyone at a jam session and would impress every teacher who has ever graded honesty. The next week, phrase two no longer sounded like a stranger who wandered into the wrong club.

Nothing mystical happened. We did not invent a new pedagogy. We removed the student’s veto on order.

What teachers already know

You already teach this. You say “start from the hard spot.” You say “don’t always begin at the top.” You write tiny numbers in the margin. Then the student goes home, and the living room votes for comfort.

The trick is not more speeches. The trick is a practice setup where each example in the exercise comes up once, with the metronome, and then the order changes. Favorites still get played. They just stop eating the whole hour.

That is what eization is for. One example on screen. Play keeps time. Next brings the next one. You can type the lines yourself from a book they already own, open a stock exercise like Scales, or send them a share link so the homework arrives as an exercise, not as a paragraph of hopes.

The punchline

M. still likes Measure 3. Everyone does. Measure 3 pays well. The difference is that Measure 3 no longer gets to decide who else shows up for work.

If your studio has a student who can play the A section until the cows come home and then looks personally betrayed by the bridge, you do not need a better pep talk. You need the bridge to come up when the click is running—and for that to keep happening after they leave your room.
